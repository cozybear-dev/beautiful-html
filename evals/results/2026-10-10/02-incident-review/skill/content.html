Engineering Post-incident review: checkout outage, 17 September 2026

One cache configuration change took checkout down for 47 minutes, and most of that time went on finding it

At 13:52 UTC the memory limit on the feature cache was lowered from 24 GB to 8 GB. Fourteen minutes later checkout began to fail, three systems away from the change, and 18,420 checkouts failed before the change was rolled back. Four follow-up actions are agreed; the first stops a slow fraud check from blocking payments at all.

More than nine in ten checkouts failed for over half an hour

Before the change, 0.4% of checkouts failed. After it, the error rate crept up to 6.7% over thirteen minutes, then jumped to 29.7% at 14:06 and passed 90% by 14:10. It stayed between 87% and 99% until the change was rolled back at 14:46, and was back at 0.4% by 14:57.

What kept working

The orders database, the token vault and the card processor all behaved normally throughout. The fault was in how long fraud scoring took, not in taking or storing card payments.

Checkout itself was never changed and never broken. It was waiting on a chain of services behind it, and the last link in that chain was the feature cache.

The failure travelled from the cache to checkout through two services that were working correctly

A card checkout passes through Checkout, Payments and Fraud before it reaches the feature cache. Fraud reads precomputed features from the cache to score each transaction. When the cache has them, a score takes about 45 ms.

Cutting the cache to a third of its memory forced it to evict most of what it held: its hit rate fell from 97% to 12% within a minute. Fraud then had to recompute features from the feature store for nearly every transaction, and its scoring time rose from 45 ms to more than 1,200 ms.

Why slow became down

Payments keeps a pool of 200 connections to Fraud and holds one for as long as a score takes. With scores taking about 27 times longer, all 200 were in use by 14:06. From then on, payment requests had nothing to wait on but each other.

Payments kept waiting for each fraud score, with no fallback when scores were slow, so a slowdown in one service became a full stop in the next. Step through the diagram to follow the order.

We were paged on the symptom, so we started three systems from the cause

The only alert that fired was on the checkout error rate, at 14:09. By then fraud scoring had been slowing for sixteen minutes and was already 35 times slower than normal. Nothing was watching it, or the cache hit rate.

The page pointed at checkout, and the visible fault was in Payments, whose connection pool was full. So the first response was to restart Payments at 14:21. The pool filled again within 90 seconds, because Payments was a victim, not the cause. It took until 14:38 to work back through Fraud to the cache and find change CHG-4471, which by then was 46 minutes old.

Once found, it was quick

Rolling back took 8 minutes and the cache needed 7 more to warm. Diagnosis took longer than detection, rollback and recovery together.

Four changes: one for each thing that let a cache setting reach customers

The incident needed four things to go wrong in a row. The change went to the whole cache at once. Nothing alerted on the cache or on Fraud. Payments kept waiting on a slow dependency, with no fallback. And nobody had seen how checkout behaves with a cold cache. Each action closes one of them.

The one that matters most

The timeout and fallback are what turn a repeat of this fault into slower fraud checks instead of an outage

The other three make a bad cache change less likely and faster to catch. Only this one removes the dependency: if Fraud has not answered in 300 ms, Payments scores by rules and carries on.

For merchants: a change on our side stopped checkout; payment and card systems were not at fault

Customer success can use the account below as it stands. It uses no internal system names.

What happened

On 17 September 2026, from 14:06 to 14:53 UTC, most card checkouts failed. We had made a configuration change to an internal system that our fraud checks rely on. It made those checks very slow, and payments queued behind them until checkout stopped responding.

What it was not

It was not an attack, and not a fault at the card processor. The systems that store orders and card tokens and that charge cards worked normally throughout. The cause was our own change, and reversing it ended the outage.

What we are changing

Checkout will no longer wait on a slow fraud check: after a fraction of a second it will fall back to a simpler check and continue. Changes of this kind will be tried on a small part of the system first and reversed automatically, and we now watch for the early signs.

Open points to settle before this goes to merchants

When the outage ended. The timeline records the error rate as below 5% at 14:53, which gives the agreed 47 minutes. The minute-by-minute metrics show 20.2% at 14:53 and the first reading below 5% at 14:57, which would make it 51 minutes. This report uses 47 throughout; the difference matters if SLA credits depend on duration.

Status of the two actions already due. The alerting action was due on 2 October and the timeout and fallback on 9 October. The source data gives due dates only, so this report does not say whether they are done. The merchant wording above assumes they are or soon will be.

What a failed checkout left behind. Checkout writes the order as well as asking Payments to authorise, and order writes kept working. The data does not say whether failed checkouts left unpaid orders that merchants can see. Merchants are likely to ask.
