The single-page checkout wins on desktop and does not help on mobile web

Recommendation: ship it on desktop and tablet now. Hold it back on mobile web until the pay button is fixed, then re-test there.

Over four weeks, 3.38% of visitors who got the new checkout placed an order, against 3.18% on the current one. That is +6.3% more orders per visitor, and the gain is real, not luck. It comes almost entirely from desktop. On mobile web, about half of all visitors, the new checkout did not help and probably converted slightly worse, which fits the known problem of the pay button sitting below the fold on short screens.

2. How sure can we be? Sure it is better; less sure by how much.

  • The direction is not in doubt. If the two checkouts really performed the same, a gap this large would turn up by chance roughly once in 50,000 tests. The new checkout was also ahead on every one of the 28 days.
  • The test itself was sound. The two groups were almost exactly the same size (284,134 and 284,037), so the split worked as designed, and the test ran its planned four full weeks.
  • The size is less certain, and the four-week average flatters it. The lift was +9.3% in the first week and settled at about +5.3% for the remaining three. For planning, use the settled figure: roughly +5%, with a likely range of +1.9% to +8.8%.

The lift started near +12% and levelled off at about +5% from the second week

Extra orders per visitor on the new checkout compared with the current one, each day of the test

An early boost that fades is common when a familiar page changes. What matters is that the lift stopped falling: it has been flat for three weeks, and is not drifting towards zero. Single days are noisy, so read the shape, not individual points.

3. Is the answer the same for everyone? No. It depends on the device.

Desktop visitors converted clearly better on the new checkout (3.61% to 4.01%). Mobile web visitors did not (2.48% to 2.42%). The gap between the two is far too large to be chance, even allowing for the fact that we looked at several groups. New and returning customers responded alike (+5.2% and +4.5%), so customer type does not change the answer.

One caution on the breakdown figures. The device and customer-type file contains 862,816 visitors and 26,254 orders, more than the 568,171 visitors and 18,625 orders in the daily file for the same test, and its overall lift is +4.9% rather than +6.3%. The cause is not yet confirmed; the breakdown may be counting people once per device or per visit. Because both checkouts are counted the same way within that file, the desktop-versus-mobile pattern holds. The breakdown numbers should not be added to, or set against, the headline numbers.

Recommendation

  1. Ship the single-page checkout to desktop and tablet now. Desktop's gain is large and certain, and tablet points the same way. Expect the desktop lift to settle somewhat below the +11.1% measured here, because that figure includes the first-week boost.
  2. Keep mobile web on the current checkout for now. Shipping everywhere today would still be a net gain overall, but it would put a page with a known defect in front of half our visitors for no measured benefit and a probable small loss.
  3. Fix the pay button on short screens, then re-test on mobile web only. If the fix brings mobile anywhere near desktop's result, that is the largest remaining upside in this project, since mobile is the biggest group.
  4. After launch, track three things for four weeks: that the desktop lift holds, average order value, and payment errors (with full counts this time).

What we still do not know

  • How much of desktop's gain is the early boost. The device breakdown is not available by day, so we cannot see desktop's settled level directly, or whether mobile got better or worse as the test went on.
  • Why the breakdown file has more visitors than the daily file (see the caution in section 3).
  • Whether the payment error difference is real. This needs the underlying counts.
  • Anything beyond four weeks, such as repeat purchases.

How the numbers were worked out

  • Conversion is orders divided by visitors, as defined in the test design. Headline figures use the daily file; device and customer-type figures use the breakdown file.
  • Likely ranges are 95% confidence intervals on the relative change. The "once in 50,000" figure comes from a standard two-sided test of two proportions (p = 0.00002). The design set the significance level at 0.05.
  • The desktop-versus-mobile difference was tested directly (p below 0.000001), so it survives correction for the handful of group comparisons made.
  • The ranges for revenue per visitor and average order value are based on the 28 day-by-day differences, since order-level revenue was not available.
  • The design planned for detecting a 3% relative lift. The whole-test range sits above that; the weeks 2–4 range does not quite, so "at least 3%" is likely but not certain.
